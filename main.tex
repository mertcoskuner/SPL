\begin{abstract}
Backdoor attacks on graph classification implant a hidden behavior through
poisoned training graphs: the trained model classifies clean graphs normally
but assigns graphs that carry an attacker's trigger to a target class. To
avoid detection, attacks keep triggers small, so that poisoned graphs resemble
clean ones. This paper asks whether structural triggers also remain
imperceptible in the spectral domain, that is, whether the normalized-Laplacian
spectrum of an individual triggered graph can be distinguished from the spectra
of clean graphs. Each graph is summarized by a fixed-length descriptor of its
spectrum, which makes graphs of different sizes comparable. Two detectors are
built on this descriptor: a clean-reference statistical test, which asks
whether a graph deviates from the natural variation of clean spectra, and a
multilayer perceptron (MLP) trained on labelled clean and triggered spectra. On
four benchmarks with three structural attacks against a graph convolutional
network, the statistical test flags most triggered graphs only on MUTAG and for
one attack on AIDS, whereas the MLP reaches ROC-AUC values of $0.84$--$1.00$.
Rejecting the graphs flagged by the MLP removes nearly all success of a learned
trigger on the three molecular benchmarks while rejecting fewer than $10\%$ of
clean graphs; on PROTEINS, substantial attack success remains. Because the MLP
is trained on examples of the attack it detects, these results measure how much
the spectrum reveals about a known attack rather than the performance of a
defense against unseen attacks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{G}{raph} neural networks~\cite{gcn,gin} are widely used to classify
graph-structured data, but they can be compromised by backdoor attacks
introduced through poisoned training data~\cite{sba2021,gta2021}. In graph
classification, an attacker embeds a trigger in selected training graphs and
relabels them with an attacker-chosen target class. The trained model then
behaves normally on clean graphs but assigns triggered graphs to the target
class. Consequently, high accuracy on clean test data cannot establish that a
model is free of a backdoor.

To avoid detection, attacks keep triggers imperceptible: they limit how much a
trigger may change a graph, so that poisoned graphs resemble clean ones, and
some attacks, such as DPSBA~\cite{dpsba2025}, explicitly constrain the
structural and semantic deviation of poisoned graphs. The attacks evaluated in
this paper, for example, confine the trigger to four nodes of the graph. A
structural trigger nevertheless rewires edges, and the eigenvalues of the
normalized graph Laplacian summarize how a graph is
connected~\cite{chung1997spectral}. A rewiring that is small in the graph may
therefore still leave a trace in its spectrum. This paper asks whether
structural triggers remain imperceptible in the spectral domain: can the
spectrum of an individual triggered graph be distinguished from the spectra of
clean graphs?

We answer this question in three steps, each building on the previous one.
First, a statistical test compares the spectrum of each graph with the
variation among clean graphs and asks whether the changes caused by a trigger
exceed this natural variation. Second, a multilayer perceptron (MLP) trained on
labelled clean and triggered spectra asks whether the spectrum as a whole
separates individual triggered graphs from clean ones, even where no single
spectral feature does. Third, graphs flagged by either detector are rejected
before they reach the trained target model, and we measure how much attack
success remains. Throughout, both detectors are compared at approximately the
same rate of rejected clean graphs.

The contributions are: (i) a clean-reference test of whether the changes in the
normalized-Laplacian spectrum caused by structural backdoor triggers exceed the
natural spectral variation among clean graphs; (ii) a spectrum-only MLP
detector for individual graphs that requires no access to the target model;
and (iii) an evaluation of how rejecting detected graphs affects attack
success. On four benchmarks, the MLP separates triggered from clean graphs far
better than the statistical test, and rejecting the graphs it flags nearly
eliminates the learned trigger on three of them; on the fourth, a substantial
part of the attack success remains.

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related attacks and defenses.
Section~\ref{sec:prelim} introduces the notation, the backdoor threat model,
the structural triggers, and the spectral representation of a graph.
Section~\ref{sec:fingerprint} presents the two detectors, and
Section~\ref{sec:numerical} reports the experiments.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
Structural graph backdoors differ in how their triggers are constructed. The
subgraph-based backdoor attack (SBA) inserts a reusable random-graph
template~\cite{sba2021}, and Motif-Backdoor inserts a motif at host nodes
chosen with the help of a model~\cite{motif}. In both, the trigger pattern is
fixed, although its placement may depend on the host graph. The graph Trojan
attack (GTA) instead learns a generator that produces a graph-specific
trigger~\cite{gta2021}, and TRAP perturbs edges guided by
gradients~\cite{10.1145/3545948.3545976}.

Backdoor attacks also differ in which parts of the graph they perturb. DPSBA
constrains the structural and semantic deviation of poisoned graphs through
distribution-aware clean-label poisoning~\cite{dpsba2025}. Other attacks leave
the structure untouched and perturb only node features: SPEAR preserves the
topology~\cite{spear2025}, and spectral-domain attacks embed the trigger in the
spectral coefficients of node-feature signals~\cite{spectralba2024}. Since such
feature-only attacks leave the adjacency matrix unchanged, they also leave the
normalized-Laplacian spectrum unchanged and are outside the scope of this
paper.

Existing graph backdoor defenses differ mainly in what they need access to.
GSP purifies training graphs by spectral low-pass filtering~\cite{gsp2025},
and RIGBD~\cite{rigbd2025} and DShield~\cite{dshield2025} inspect the possibly
poisoned training data and intervene during training. DMGNN prunes triggers
using counterfactual explanations of the model~\cite{dmgnn2024}, and GraphProt
avoids access to model internals but queries the classifier repeatedly on
sampled subgraphs~\cite{graphprot2025}. A2GBD combines active node selection
with reinforcement learning to avoid assumptions about the
attack~\cite{a2gbd2026}. SSP also relies on an eigenanalysis, but of the
feature covariance and combined with neighborhood feature
deviations~\cite{ssp2026}, not of the graph Laplacian. The detector studied
here needs less: it reads only the normalized-Laplacian spectrum of an
incoming graph. Once trained, it requires neither the training set of the
target model nor its parameters, gradients, or outputs, and it neither
modifies the input graph nor retrains the model; it acts as a filter in front
of the model. Its training, however, requires triggered examples of the
attack, a requirement discussed in Section~\ref{sec:mlp}.

\section{Preliminaries and Problem Formulation}\label{sec:prelim}

\subsection{Graph Classification}
A graph $G=(\calV,\calE,\bX)$ has $n=|\calV|$ nodes, an edge set $\calE$, an
adjacency matrix $\bA\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}$, a diagonal degree matrix $\bD$
whose entry $d_v$ is the number of neighbors of node $v$, and a feature matrix
$\bX$ whose row $\mathbf{x}_v$ is the feature vector of node $v$. Let $\calG$
be the set of such graphs and $\calC$ a set of $C$ class labels. A graph
classifier $f_\theta\colon\calG\to\Delta^{C-1}$ with parameters $\theta$ maps a
graph to a vector of class probabilities, where $\Delta^{C-1}$ is the set of
probability vectors of length $C$, and predicts the label
$h_\theta(G)=\arg\max_{c\in\calC} f_{\theta,c}(G)$.

\subsection{Holm Procedure}\label{sec:holm}
The statistical detector of Section~\ref{sec:stat} tests each graph at many
coordinates of its spectral descriptor at once. Even for a clean graph, some of
these tests would reject by chance, so the tests of one graph are corrected for
multiplicity with Holm's step-down procedure~\cite{holm1979}. Given $m$
$p$-values sorted in increasing order, the procedure compares the smallest with
$\alpha/m$, the second smallest with $\alpha/(m-1)$, and so on, and rejects the
hypotheses up to the first comparison that fails. For valid $p$-values, the
probability of rejecting at least one true hypothesis is then at most $\alpha$,
without requiring the tests to be independent. In this paper, only whether a
graph has at least one rejection matters. This is decided by the first
comparison alone: some hypothesis is rejected exactly when the smallest
$p$-value is at most $\alpha/m$, and the smallest Holm-adjusted $p$-value is
$m$ times the smallest $p$-value, capped at $1$.

\subsection{Backdoor Trojan}
\subsubsection{Basics of a Backdoor Trojan}
A backdoor Trojan (backdoor, for short) is a hidden behavior implanted in a
model through poisoned training data and activated by a trigger, a pattern
that the attacker inserts into an input graph. The attacker describes the
insertion by a trigger policy $T\colon\calG\times\calC\to\calG$, which maps a
graph $G$ and a target label $y_t\in\calC$ to the triggered graph
$\tilde G=T(G,y_t)$.

\begin{definition}[Backdoor attack]
Let $\mathcal{P}$ be a distribution over $\calG\times\calC$, let $f_\theta$ be a
classifier trained on clean samples from $\mathcal{P}$, and let
$\delta_a,\delta_c\in[0,1)$ be tolerances. A backdoor attack with target label
$y_t$ and trigger policy $T$ produces a poisoned classifier $f_{\theta^*}$ that
satisfies
\begin{align*}
\Pr_{(G,y)\sim\mathcal{P}}\bigl[\,h_{\theta^*}\bigl(T(G,y_t)\bigr)=y_t
  \;\big|\; y\neq y_t \,\bigr] &\ \ge\ 1-\delta_a,\\
\Pr_{(G,y)\sim\mathcal{P}}\bigl[\,h_{\theta^*}(G)=h_{\theta}(G)\,\bigr]
  &\ \ge\ 1-\delta_c .
\end{align*}
The first condition is the backdoor behavior on triggered inputs, and the
second is the preservation of clean behavior.
\end{definition}

\noindent
The empirical counterparts of the two conditions, the attack success rate and
the clean accuracy measured on test graphs, are defined in
Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

\subsubsection{Backdoor Insertion Framework}\label{sec:policy}
A backdoor attack proceeds in three stages~\cite{sba2021,gta2021}: the attacker
designs the trigger policy, poisons part of the training data with it, and
releases the data to the owner of the target model, whose training implants
the backdoor. Let $\calD=\{(G_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ be the clean training set.

\paragraph{Trigger-Insertion Policy Design}
Imperceptibility enters at this stage. With a surrogate classifier
$f_{\hat\theta}$, the attacker seeks a policy that triggers the target label
while changing each graph only slightly:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\max_{T}\;\; & \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
  \mathbf{1}\bigl[h_{\hat\theta}(T(G_i,y_t))=y_t\bigr]\\
\text{s.t.}\;\; & d\bigl(T(G_i,y_t),G_i\bigr)\le\epsilon,\quad i=1,\dots,N,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:attack-design}
\end{equation}
where $d$ measures how much a trigger changes a graph and $\epsilon$ bounds this
change. When $T$ is a neural network $T_\phi$ with weights $\phi$, problem
\eqref{eq:attack-design} can be relaxed by replacing the constraint with a
penalty $\gamma\,d$ and the indicator with the cross-entropy loss, so that
$\phi$ can be trained by gradient descent. The trained generator then produces
the trigger for a new graph in a single forward pass. In either form, $d$ is
measured on the graph itself. The question of this paper is whether a trigger
that changes the graph only slightly also leaves its spectrum nearly unchanged.

\paragraph{Data Poisoning}
The attacker applies the policy to a subset $\calD_p\subset\calD$ containing a
fraction $\rho$ of the training graphs, relabels these graphs with $y_t$, and
releases the poisoned training set
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\calD}=(\calD\setminus\calD_p)\cup
\bigl\{(T(G,y_t),\,y_t) : (G,y)\in\calD_p\bigr\}.
\label{eq:poisoned-set}
\end{equation}
The attacker aims to keep the clean accuracy of the resulting model while
increasing its attack success; this trade-off is evaluated empirically.

\paragraph{Model Poisoning}
The owner of the target model trains it on $\widetilde{\calD}$ as released,
with its usual training loss and without any control by the attacker, and
obtains the poisoned parameters $\theta^*$.

\subsection{Trigger Insertion in Graphs}\label{sec:insertion}
The three attacks evaluated here modify the structure of a graph inside a small
region $\calV_g\subseteq\calV$ of $k=|\calV_g|$ nodes, the trigger size. The
trigger removes a set of edges $\calE_{\mathrm{cut}}\subseteq\calE$ and inserts
a set $\calE_g$, so that the triggered graph $\tilde G$ has the edge set
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\calE}=(\calE\setminus\calE_{\mathrm{cut}})\cup\calE_g,
\label{eq:edge-composition}
\end{equation}
the same node set $\calV$, and possibly modified features on $\calV_g$. Attacks
on node classification such as UGBA~\cite{ugba2022} and
DPGBA~\cite{dpgba2023} instead inject new trigger nodes; the attacks evaluated
here all select existing nodes and therefore preserve $n$. They differ in how
they choose $\calE_{\mathrm{cut}}$ and $\calE_g$.

\subsubsection{Static Triggers}\label{sec:static}
SBA and Motif-Backdoor insert a fixed pattern $\Gamma=(\calV_\Gamma,\calE_\Gamma)$:
an Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi random graph for SBA~\cite{sba2021}, and for
Motif-Backdoor the motif M44, a triangle with a pendant node
attached~\cite{motif}. A placement map $\iota\colon\calV_\Gamma\to\calV$ chooses
the host nodes $\calV_g=\iota(\calV_\Gamma)$, uniformly at random for SBA and by
a model-guided ranking for Motif-Backdoor (Appendix~\ref{app:training}). The
edges among the host nodes are replaced by the pattern, that is,
$\calE_{\mathrm{cut}}=\calE[\calV_g]$, the edges with both endpoints in
$\calV_g$, and $\calE_g=\iota(\calE_\Gamma)$. Edges between the host nodes and
the rest of the graph, as well as all features, are unchanged. The pattern is
fixed, but its effect on the spectrum can still vary with the host graph and
the placement.

\subsubsection{Dynamic Triggers}\label{sec:dynamic}
GTA~\cite{gta2021} generates a trigger that depends on the host graph. For a
selected region $\calV_g$, a topology generator proposes edges between nodes of
$\calV_g$, which are added to the graph, and a feature generator perturbs the
features of these nodes:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\bA}=\max\bigl(\bA,\,\bA_\phi(G)\bigr),\qquad
\tilde{\bX}=\bX+\bX_\phi(G).
\label{eq:dynamic-trigger}
\end{equation}
Here $\bA_\phi(G)$ is a binary adjacency matrix with nonzero entries only
between nodes of $\calV_g$, the maximum is taken entry by entry, and
$\bX_\phi(G)$ is nonzero only in the rows of $\calV_g$. GTA therefore adds edges
($\calE_{\mathrm{cut}}=\varnothing$) and changes features; the choice of
$\calV_g$ itself is not learned.

All three attacks thus change the edges of the graph. The spectral
representation introduced next depends only on the edges: it reflects
$\calE_{\mathrm{cut}}$ and $\calE_g$ but not the feature perturbation of GTA.

\subsection{Graphs in Spectral Domain}\label{sec:spectral}
The normalized Laplacian of $G$ is
\begin{equation}
\bL=\bI-\bD^{-1/2}\bA\bD^{-1/2}.
\label{eq:laplacian}
\end{equation}
It is symmetric, so it has $n$ real eigenvalues
$0\le\lambda_1\le\dots\le\lambda_n\le 2$ with orthonormal
eigenvectors~\cite{chung1997spectral}. We call the vector
$\blam(G)=[\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n]^{\top}$ the spectrum of $G$.

An eigenvector $\bu$ of $\bL$ assigns a value
$u_v$ to every node $v$. For a graph without isolated nodes and an eigenvector
of unit length, the corresponding eigenvalue is
\begin{equation}
\lambda=\bu^{\top}\bL\bu=\sum_{\{v,w\}\in\calE}
\Bigl(\frac{u_v}{\sqrt{d_v}}-\frac{u_w}{\sqrt{d_w}}\Bigr)^{2}.
\label{eq:quadratic-form}
\end{equation}
An eigenvalue is therefore small when the values of its eigenvector, divided by
the square root of the node degrees, are similar at the two ends of every
edge, and large when they differ. In the language of graph signal
processing~\cite{shuman2013gsp}, the eigenvectors are the oscillation modes of
the graph and the eigenvalues their frequencies.

The small eigenvalues, or low frequencies,
describe how the graph is connected. An eigenvalue $0$ arises from an
eigenvector that is constant (up to the degree scaling) on a connected
component, so the number of zero eigenvalues equals the number of connected
components, isolated nodes aside. For a connected graph, a small $\lambda_2$
indicates a bottleneck: the graph can be split into two sizeable parts by
removing comparatively few edges~\cite{chung1997spectral}. The large
eigenvalues, or high frequencies, belong to eigenvectors whose values alternate
between neighboring nodes. The largest possible value, $2$, occurs exactly when
a connected component is bipartite, that is, when its nodes can be divided into
two groups such that every edge joins the two
groups~\cite{chung1997spectral}. The spectrum does not determine a graph
uniquely, but it summarizes its connectivity: how many separate pieces it has,
whether it has a bottleneck, and whether it is bipartite. A trigger that
rewires the edges among a few nodes may change this connectivity locally, and
with it some eigenvalues.

The spectrum has one eigenvalue per node, so
graphs of different size have spectra of different length that cannot be
compared entry by entry. We therefore place the eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ at its
relative position $r_i=(i-1)/(n-1)$ in the sorted spectrum, so that the first
and last eigenvalues are at $0$ and $1$ for every $n\ge2$, and connect the
points $(r_i,\lambda_i)$ by straight lines. The resulting nondecreasing curve
$\Lambda_G\colon[0,1]\to[0,2]$ keeps the shape of the spectrum but not its
length. Reading it at $K$ equally spaced positions gives the spectral
descriptor
\begin{equation}
\bs(G)=\bigl[\Lambda_G(1/K),\,\Lambda_G(2/K),\,\dots,\,\Lambda_G(1)\bigr]^{\top}
\in[0,2]^{K},
\label{eq:specvec}
\end{equation}
with coordinates $s_j(G)=\Lambda_G(j/K)$. Position $0$ is omitted because
$\Lambda_G(0)=\lambda_1=0$ for every graph with at least one edge. Since the
eigenvalues do not depend on the order in which the nodes are numbered, neither
does $\bs(G)$, so graphs can be compared without matching their nodes; and
since every graph is mapped to the same $K$ positions, graphs of different
size can be compared coordinate by coordinate. The first coordinates describe
the low-frequency, connectivity end of the spectrum, and the last coordinates
the high-frequency end, with $s_K(G)=\lambda_n$. When $n-1<K$, the positions
fall between eigenvalues and neighboring coordinates interpolate between the
same two eigenvalues, so they are strongly dependent; when $n-1>K$, the
positions skip some eigenvalues. The number of positions $K$ is a free
parameter of the method; the value used in the experiments is given in
Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

\section{Fingerprint of Backdoor Trojan}\label{sec:fingerprint}
We call the change that a trigger makes to the descriptor~\eqref{eq:specvec}
its spectral fingerprint. A defender observes only the incoming graph, not its
clean version, so the fingerprint cannot be measured as the difference
$\bs(\tilde G)-\bs(G)$. Detection must instead decide whether the descriptor of
the observed graph lies outside the range of clean descriptors. The detector
acts at inference time, as a filter in front of an already trained model: a
graph that it flags is rejected before it reaches the model. The poisoned
training set is not cleaned, and the model is not retrained. We consider two
detectors that differ in the data they need. The statistical detector
(Section~\ref{sec:stat}) uses clean graphs only and asks, coordinate by
coordinate, whether a graph deviates more than clean graphs do. The
model-based detector (Section~\ref{sec:mlp}), which is the proposed method,
additionally uses triggered examples and learns one decision over all
coordinates.

\subsection{Statistical Detection of Backdoor Trojan}\label{sec:stat}
Let $\calR$ be a reference set of clean graphs, and let $\mu_j$ and $\sigma_j$ be
the sample mean and standard deviation of the coordinate $s_j$ over $\calR$.
Coordinates that are constant over $\calR$ carry no information and are
discarded, which leaves $m\le K$ coordinates. For an observed graph $G$, each
remaining coordinate is compared with the clean variation through a two-sided
$p$-value under a Gaussian approximation,
\begin{equation}
p_j(G)=2\Bigl[1-\Phi\Bigl(\frac{|s_j(G)-\mu_j|}{\sigma_j}\Bigr)\Bigr],
\label{eq:clean-reference-test}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. The $m$
$p$-values of $G$ are corrected with the Holm procedure at level $\alpha$
(Section~\ref{sec:holm}), which does not require the tests to be independent;
this matters here because neighboring coordinates are strongly dependent. The
detector flags $G$, $\hat b_{\mathrm{stat}}(G)=1$, if at least one coordinate is
rejected, that is, if the smallest $p$-value of $G$ is at most $\alpha/m$.

The detector uses no triggered graphs. Because the Gaussian model is only an
approximation, however, $\alpha$ does not fix the fraction of clean graphs that
are flagged; this fraction is measured on test data in
Section~\ref{sec:numerical}.

\subsection{Model-Based Backdoor Detection}\label{sec:mlp}
The statistical detector examines each coordinate on its own, so a fingerprint
that consists of small deviations spread over many coordinates can go
unnoticed. The model-based detector instead learns one decision over the whole
descriptor. It is trained on clean graphs and their triggered versions,
labelled $b=0$ and $b=1$, respectively. Each coordinate of the descriptor is
standardized with the mean and standard deviation of the training graphs,
which gives $\bar\bs(G)$, and an MLP $g_\psi$ with parameters $\psi$ is trained
with the binary cross-entropy loss to output the detection score
$q(G)=g_\psi\bigl(\bar\bs(G)\bigr)$. The detector flags $G$ when its score
exceeds a threshold $\tau$:
\begin{equation}
\hat b_{\mathrm{MLP}}(G)=
\begin{cases}
1, & q(G)>\tau,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:mlp-detector}
\end{equation}
The threshold is set on held-out clean graphs so that at most a fraction
$\beta$ of them is flagged. The budget $\beta$ thus fixes the cost of the
detector on clean inputs; setting it to the clean rejection rate of the
statistical detector lets the two detectors be compared at the same cost
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Logistic regression on $\bar\bs(G)$ serves as a
linear reference: if the MLP separates the graphs better, part of the
information lies in nonlinear combinations of coordinates.

Training requires triggered examples of the attack to be detected, and a
separate detector is trained for each dataset and attack. A defender does not
normally hold such examples. The model-based results therefore show how much a
detector can extract from the descriptor when the attack is known; they do not
describe a defense against unseen attacks, and transfer across attacks is not
studied in this paper.

The detectors of a fold are built only from the graphs outside
its test split, called the pool, so no test graph influences a detector. The
descriptor uses $K=100$ positions. This value is a fixed design choice; its
influence is not studied in this paper. Since at least $93\%$ of the graphs in
every dataset have $n\le K$ nodes, the descriptor interpolates between
eigenvalues for most graphs, which is the case of strongly dependent
neighboring coordinates noted in Section~\ref{sec:spectral}. The reference set
$\calR$ of the statistical detector contains all clean pool graphs. For the
logistic regression and the MLP, the pool is split by source graph, so that a
clean graph and its triggered version always fall on the same side; the first
part provides the training pairs, and the clean graphs of the second part set
the threshold $\tau$. The budget $\beta$ is the rate at which the statistical
detector flags clean pool graphs, each scored against a reference set from
which it is left out. Both detectors therefore operate at approximately the
same cost on clean graphs.

Triggered test graphs are obtained by applying the trigger to
the clean test graphs, so the two sets differ only by the trigger. A triggered
graph is \emph{eligible} if its true label differs from $y_t$ and the trigger
can be applied to it. The clean accuracy (CA) is the accuracy of the poisoned
model on clean test graphs, and the attack success rate (ASR) is the fraction
of eligible triggered test graphs that it assigns to $y_t$. For a detector, the
false-positive rate (FPR) is the fraction of clean test graphs it flags, and
the true-positive rate (TPR) is the fraction of eligible triggered test graphs
it flags. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve
(ROC-AUC) measures how well a detector's score ranks triggered above clean
graphs over all thresholds; $0.5$ corresponds to chance and $1$ to perfect
separation. The score is $q(G)$ for the MLP, the corresponding output for
logistic regression, and the smallest Holm-adjusted $p$-value for the
statistical detector. For the defense, every eligible triggered test graph is
first passed to the detector, and the graphs it flags are rejected. The
post-detection ASR is the fraction of \emph{all} eligible triggered test graphs
that are accepted and assigned to $y_t$ by the same poisoned model; a rejected
graph counts as a failed attack. It is measured by running the model on the
accepted graphs rather than derived from the TPR, because the triggered graphs
that a detector misses need not be those on which the attack succeeds.

\subsubsection{Datasets}
AIDS, MUTAG, and NCI1 contain small molecules and are called the molecular
datasets below. PROTEINS contains protein structures; its graphs are larger and
have a higher average degree. All four come from the TUDataset
benchmark~\cite{tudataset}; Appendix~\ref{app:structure} summarizes their
structure (Table~\ref{tab:dataset_stats_graph}).

\subsubsection{Backdoor Trojan Attacks}
Each dataset is poisoned with SBA and Motif-Backdoor (static triggers,
Section~\ref{sec:static}) and with GTA (dynamic trigger,
Section~\ref{sec:dynamic}), at the poisoning rate and trigger size of
Table~\ref{tab:setup}. All three modify existing nodes; none adds nodes to a
graph. None of the attacks is adapted to the detectors.

\subsubsection{Statistical Detection}
This part asks whether a trigger moves the descriptor beyond the variation of
clean graphs. Since each triggered test graph differs from a clean test graph
only by its trigger, a TPR of the statistical detector above its FPR means that
this happens for the flagged graphs. The multiple-testing correction is
essential for this reading. Without it, the coordinatewise test flags
$30.8\%$--$64.3\%$ of clean test graphs, because each graph is tested at many
coordinates; with the Holm correction, the clean rejection falls to
$3.1\%$--$9.2\%$. At this cost, the detector flags most triggered graphs only
on MUTAG ($68.8\%$--$96.8\%$) and under GTA on AIDS ($76.0\%$); on NCI1, GTA also
gives its highest TPR, $42.5\%$. Under SBA and Motif-Backdoor on AIDS and NCI1,
it flags $15.6\%$--$32.8\%$ of triggered graphs, which is $2.3$ to $5.7$ times its
clean rejection. On PROTEINS, the TPR of $7.6\%$--$11.8\%$ is $2.5$ to $3.9$ times
the FPR of $3.1\%$, but the ROC-AUC remains low, $0.553$--$0.603$ against $0.5$
for chance. Measured one coordinate at a time against the clean variation, the
trigger is thus visible for most triggered graphs only on MUTAG and under GTA
on AIDS; in the remaining settings, most triggered graphs have no single
coordinate that deviates enough to survive the correction. Whether the
descriptor as a whole carries more information is the subject of the next
part.

\subsubsection{MLP Detection Performance}
The model-based detectors show that the trigger leaves a clearer fingerprint
than the coordinatewise test reveals. The MLP reaches ROC-AUC values of
$0.844$--$1.000$ and, at its calibrated threshold, flags $34.2\%$--$100.0\%$ of
triggered graphs at an FPR of $3.1\%$--$9.7\%$; at matched clean cost, its TPR
exceeds that of the statistical detector in every setting. Part of this gain
comes from supervision alone: logistic regression already exceeds the ROC-AUC
of the statistical detector in every setting. The MLP improves on logistic
regression in $9$ of the $12$ settings, most under Motif-Backdoor on PROTEINS
($0.761$ to $0.844$) and NCI1 ($0.935$ to $0.990$), so part of the information
lies in nonlinear combinations of coordinates. On MUTAG, both separate the
graphs almost perfectly, and logistic regression is slightly higher under SBA;
the MLP does not uniformly dominate the linear reference.

PROTEINS remains the most difficult dataset. At an FPR of $3.1\%$--$3.2\%$, the
MLP flags $34.2\%$--$49.8\%$ of triggered graphs, against $7.6\%$--$11.8\%$ for
the statistical detector; logistic regression reaches ROC-AUC
$0.761$--$0.842$, and the MLP adds $0.04$--$0.08$. Since no single coordinate
separates most triggered PROTEINS graphs from clean ones, weak deviations
spread over the descriptor remain jointly informative once labelled examples
are available. PROTEINS graphs are also larger and denser than the molecular
ones (Table~\ref{tab:dataset_stats_graph}), so a four-node trigger changes a
smaller part of the graph. This is a possible contributor to the weaker
detection, not a cause established by these experiments.

\subsubsection{Analyzing Overall Defense Capability}
The last column group of Table~\ref{tab:results} shows the effect of rejecting
flagged graphs, and it follows the separability reported above. Under GTA, the
MLP lowers the ASR from $0.999$ to $0.001$ on AIDS, from $1.000$ to $0.000$ on
MUTAG, and from $1.000$ to $0.003$ on NCI1, whereas the statistical detector
leaves $0.240$, $0.032$, and $0.575$, respectively. On PROTEINS under GTA,
rejection lowers the ASR from $0.951$ to $0.844$ with the statistical detector
and to $0.462$ with the MLP, so substantial backdoor behavior remains; under
SBA and Motif-Backdoor on PROTEINS, the post-detection ASR stays between
$0.300$ and $0.489$. On AIDS and MUTAG, SBA and Motif-Backdoor succeed rarely
even without defense, so the absolute reductions there are small.

The post-detection ASR of the MLP is no higher than that of the statistical
detector in every setting, and the two are equal only on MUTAG under
Motif-Backdoor, where both reach $0.000$. This comparison is descriptive: the
five folds are not independent experiments, and the FPRs of the two detectors
differ by less than one percentage point. Rejection thus nearly removes the
backdoor where clean and triggered descriptors are well separated, and its
benefit shrinks as they overlap.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper examined whether structural backdoor triggers in graph
classification remain imperceptible in the spectral domain. The answer depends
on how the spectrum is examined. Tested one coordinate at a time against the
variation of clean graphs, the trigger is visible for most triggered graphs
only on MUTAG and under GTA on AIDS. Learned jointly over the whole
descriptor, the fingerprint is much clearer: on the molecular benchmarks, a
spectrum-only MLP separates triggered from clean graphs with ROC-AUC between
$0.91$ and $1.00$ and, when used to reject inputs, removes nearly all success of
the learned GTA trigger on AIDS, MUTAG, and NCI1 while rejecting
$6.3\%$--$9.7\%$ of clean graphs. On PROTEINS, both detectors are less
effective, and substantial attack success remains after rejection.

Three limitations bound these findings. First, the MLP is trained on triggered
graphs of the attack it detects, so its results measure what the spectrum
reveals about a known attack rather than describe a defense against unseen
attacks; transfer across attacks is not addressed. Second, the attacks are not
adapted to the detectors, and only a GCN target model and structural triggers
are considered. Third, no non-spectral baseline is compared, so the detectors
may respond to any structural change that the spectrum reflects, such as a
changed number of edges. Detecting triggers without labelled examples, and
against attackers that adapt to the detector, remains open.

\section{Training Settings}
\label{app:training}
The five folds are stratified, and within each fold a nested stratified
split reserves $10\%$ of the full dataset for validation. The split seed is
$12345$ and the model seed is $0$. The reported results use a GCN target
model at trigger size $k=4$; runs with GraphSAGE~\cite{graphsage} and
GAT~\cite{gat} target models and at other trigger sizes are not included in
this paper.

The graph-attack task file configures SBA with an Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi pattern
and Motif-Backdoor with the M44 motif. The run records store the shared budget
and target settings but not the remaining attack parameters, which are
implementation defaults: SBA edge probability $0.5$, Motif-Backdoor candidate
pool of $10$ nodes, and, for GTA, two-hop adjacency and aggregated node
features as generator input, topology threshold $0.5$, feature threshold $0$,
and generator learning rate $0.01$. SBA places its pattern on uniformly
sampled host nodes. Motif-Backdoor ranks high-degree candidate nodes by how
much the surrogate logits change when their incident edges are removed. For
GTA, the generated adjacency is binarized with the topology threshold and
restricted to pairs of nodes in the selected region. For a graph with fewer
than $k$ nodes, SBA inserts the pattern induced on the available nodes if
there are at least two; otherwise, and for Motif-Backdoor and GTA, the graph is
left unchanged.

For an isolated node, the corresponding entry of $\bD^{-1/2}$
in~\eqref{eq:laplacian} is set to zero, so that each isolated node contributes
an eigenvalue of $1$. Coordinates whose clean standard deviation over $\calR$
is below $10^{-6}$ are treated as constant and discarded, and $10^{-6}$ is added
to $\sigma_j$ in~\eqref{eq:clean-reference-test} for numerical stability.
